# Judge: The Designer

## 1. Who I am

I read these as printed matter first and motion second. I look at the grid, the type, the palette, the print process, and whether the piece understands the movement it borrows from or only its surface. I give real credit for craft and none for effects.

> **Coverage note (read this first).** The coordinator asked me to stop before I had finished. I looked closely at the contact sheets for **44 of the 72 scenes**: all 22 Posters, the rest of batch 04, the sketch batch (sk) and the nine originals. I did **not** open the sheets for the 28 scenes from batches 01 to 03: Planets, Flyover, Dot Matrix, Current, Coral, Attractor, Magnetosphere, Interference, Abyss, Aurora, Skyline, Rave, Oil, Mandala, Descent, Murmuration, Bloom, Iris, Night Train, Sumi, Stargate, Scanlines, Chladni, Physarum, Expressway, Nebula, Paper and Terminal. I will not invent scores for work I have not seen, so those rows are marked **n/s** (not seen), and nothing below is based on them. I also judged every sheet at contact-sheet scale. I opened no full-size 1280×720 frames, so any remark about kerning or ink squeeze is as much as a 480 px tile can show.

## 3. My top 10 (of the 44 seen)

1. **Constructivist.** It understands that Rodchenko's diagonal is structural, not decorative: the whole banner runs at about 25° and the words crop at the edges, so the eye completes them. The megaphone wedge, the halftone speaker and the red record rhyme as circle, wedge and bar. The kick lands in the figures and the words, not across the page. The small caption type ("A NIGHT OF SOUND AND MOVEMENT") is placed like a real imprint.
2. **Republic (TDR).** Of all 22 homages it is the most fluent. There are several levels of type hierarchy, numbered index lists, a fake corporate glyph, katakana set vertically, and a scrolling crawl at the foot. The drop reverses the headline to acid on black, which is exactly how TDR would have handled emphasis. It moves like a machine, which is the whole idea of the style.
3. **Zagreb.** Limited animation: few inks, a textured paper border, and a studio credit strip. The drop does not simply add glow; it adds a tram, a chorus line and a red coat. Narrative is the reaction, and that is rare in this collection.
4. **Poster.** Swiss rigour: a vertical KLANG, a single red, a rule, and small metadata type in the margin. On the drop the layout re-sets instead of shaking, and the marquee band is a good second typographic register. The flaw is that the "NAC" crop in the breakdown looks accidental rather than intended.
5. **De Stijl.** Tilting the grid 45° on the drop is the one move that proves the author read van Doesburg. The side column carries a real programme list with checkboxes that light up in turn: information design driven by the music.
6. **Cubist.** The oval format, the stencilled VIN and the newspaper fragment are all present and correctly ranked. The drop adds colour facets (wood grain, rose), the way synthetic cubism grew out of analytic.
7. **Penny Dreadful.** Two columns of body text the reader was never meant to read, a price, and several faces in the masthead. The phantom turning red on the drop and the diagonal "GRAND DOUBLE NUMBER" band are a pulp editor's instincts.
8. **Mobile.** The best restraint in the set. Three primaries and black on a white wall, lit so the shadows do half the work. The drop is more arms, never more light.
9. **Orphism.** The palette is full but matte, and the disc is the only rhythm. The lettered grid on the right (each glyph in its own coloured cell) is a smart, original typographic answer.
10. **Plate.** A naturalist's plate with correct radial symmetry, an engraved border and small italic captions. The drop populates the plate instead of flashing, as a scientific plate would.

(Honourable mentions: Direct Address, WPA, Futurist, Thresholds, Suprematism and Zine.)

## 4. Weakest 8 (of the 44 seen)

1. **Arcs (1).** A small crescent in the middle of 640×360 pixels of black. Fix: treat it as a Josef Müller-Brockmann concert poster. Put the arcs on a grid of several concentric sets, let each beat draw a quadrant in a different weight, and add a single line of type that anchors the page.
2. **Text (2).** "VIZ" in dotted white, turned into blobs on the drop. Fix: this should be the house typographic scene. Set a real word in a real face at poster scale, and let the kick spread the ink (letterpress squash) instead of inflating the letters.
3. **Rings (2).** It occupies about 4% of the frame. Fix: scale the rings up until they run past the edges, then treat them like the grooves of a record, printed in two inks and offset like Riley's circles.
4. **Parametric Lines (2).** Invisible for most of the track, then neon on black. Fix: make it an engraved spirograph. Guilloché in one ink on security-paper green, the pattern a banknote uses, with the drop adding a second ink in register.
5. **Jags (3).** A palette looking for a composition. Fix: turn it into a 1950s textile or a Rosalind Russell-era barkcloth border, with a repeat, a margin and a selvedge label, so the drop adds a second pattern band.
6. **Twinkle Toph (3).** The same portrait in every frame. Fix: make it a Warhol grid of four or nine panels, a different two-ink combination in each, with the kick changing one panel's inks.
7. **Iso City (5).** Colour mud and no hierarchy. Fix: limit it to three inks (for example grey, blue and a single golden tower), which would make the drop's rising tower the unmistakable subject. Pull back the camera, or show fewer blocks.
8. **Clay (5).** It is pleasant but has no point of view. Fix: commit to one reference, whether Aardman lighting, Morph or a Czech puppet film. Lower the saturation, use one warm key light with real cast shadows, and give the scene a single character action on the drop instead of more characters.

## 5. The Posters series

As a set, the 22 are the strongest body of work I have seen here. The authors clearly read the brief, invented original copy, and mostly let the music reset the layout instead of shaking it.

- **Understood:** Constructivist, TDR, Zagreb, De Stijl, Cubist, Penny Dreadful, Orphism, WPA, Futurist, Direct Address, Suprematism and Zine. In each, the movement's structural idea (the diagonal, the grid, limited animation, simultaneous contrast, parole in libertà, direct address) is doing the work, not only its colours.
- **Partly understood:** Mass Poster has the right silhouettes but a static composition; Soviet posters were built on low-angle perspective and crowd scale. Diner has good neon but a flat front elevation. Glitch has good control, but the poster underneath is too generic. Letterpress has a lovely process story, but its breakdown pages are empty. The Magazine Cover has a good masthead but a vector, not gouache, finish.
- **Superficial:** Vorticism has the right text (HAIL/SCORN) but thin shards; the vortex should consume the page. Liquid Glass is a rendering demo, not a poster. Low Poly is a generic desktop wallpaper. Grunge is murky where Carson was precise even when illegible.

**What the series is missing:**
- Swiss International Style as its own entry. Poster sits close, but a proper Müller-Brockmann grid poster would anchor the series.
- A true risograph or screen-print misregistration piece, with a visible offset between inks. Only WPA flirts with it.
- Anything from the Polish poster school, or Japanese modernism: Tanaka, Nagai or Yokoo.
- Psychedelic concert posters (Wes Wilson, Moscoso), the obvious music-poster tradition and the one legitimate home for vibrating colour.
- Blue Note record sleeves (Reid Miles), where type is the rhythm.
- A series-level voice. Every poster uses the same test copy about dancing and night. A common typographic device, such as a consistent numbered folio ("Nº 4 of 22"), would make it read as a portfolio.

## 6. Themes

1. **Print wins.** The best work in the 44 is paper, ink and a limited palette, and TASTE.md's range turn after batch 03 has paid off.
2. **The layout re-sets instead of shaking.** De Stijl, Poster, Direct Address and TDR change their layout on the drop, which is more dignified and more legible than zoom punches.
3. **Narrative drops.** Zagreb's crowd, Impasto's crows and Suprematism's flight each add a cast of characters on the drop. That reads well across a room.
4. **Headlines that change with the section.** WPA, Cubist, Letterpress and Mass Poster each have a headline for the intro, one for the drop and one for the breakdown. It works as a structure and should be the norm.
5. **Weak originals.** The nine originals (Text to Dot Matrix, of which I saw six) are sketches on black and stand far below everything later.
6. **Glow and a centred subject still linger** in Ophanim and in parts of Diner, while the matte scenes read better on a projector.
7. **The breakdown is often dead air.** Letterpress's blank pages and the empty frames in Parametric Lines show that an exhale still needs something on the page.

## 7. Transplants

1. **The programme list from De Stijl → Poster, Suprematism and Orphism,** because a side column of events whose checkboxes light on the beat turns the music into information design.
2. **The headline per section (WPA and Cubist) → Vorticism, Low Poly, Liquid Glass and Diner,** because it gives the track a legible arc without global flashes.
3. **The breakdown that thins to white in Suprematism → Letterpress,** because the page should fade gently rather than go blank.
4. **The scrolling marquee band from Poster and TDR → Mass Poster and Glitch,** because a moving typographic register carries the hats without disturbing the main composition.
5. **The crowd arriving on the drop in Zagreb → Mass Poster and Magazine Cover,** because narrative bodies arriving is the most legible drop there is.
6. **Mobile's real cast shadows on a pale wall → Cube, Stamps and Clay,** because the shadow is a quiet second layer that moves without adding glow.
7. **The dual register of blind deboss then inked print (Letterpress) → Penny Dreadful and WPA,** because a build that starts as a latent impression and inks on the drop is a print-true way to show the build.
8. **The misregistered second ink in Zine (pink) → Constructivist and Mass Poster,** because a slight offset between the red and black passes on the kick is period-true and confined.

## 8. New ideas

1. **Swiss Grid (Müller-Brockmann, "der Film", Zürich Tonhalle).** A strict 8-column grid, circles in one or two inks, and Akzidenz-style type. The kick moves one circle to the next grid position, the bass scales the arcs, and the drop introduces a second colour and a counter-rotating ring set. This is the correct home for Arcs and Rings.
2. **Psychedelic Fillmore (Wes Wilson, Victor Moscoso).** Letterforms that melt to fill a silhouette, set in vibrating complementary pairs. The bass swells the letters, the snare swaps the pair (red/green, then orange/blue), and the drop brings concentric Moscoso outlines. It is the one place where vibration is historically correct.
3. **Blue Note sleeve (Reid Miles).** Type as rhythm: huge condensed words cropped at the edge, a tinted photo, and three flat inks. Each bar re-sets the word stack like a drummer's fill, and the drop reverses the whole layout out of black.
4. **Polish Poster School (Lenica, Tomaszewski, Cieślewicz).** A single painted surreal head, hand-lettered, one colour on a paper ground. The kick opens an eye or a mouth, and the drop splits the head into a second image.
5. **Riso Zine Duotone.** Two drums, fluorescent pink and blue, with visible misregistration that the music shifts. The kick nudges the pink plate, and the drop adds a third drum in yellow with a moiré from overprinting.
6. **Japanese modernism (Ikko Tanaka, Kazumasa Nagai).** A few flat geometric planes forming a face or a figure in a Nihon Buyo pose. The planes rotate one at a time, a hat ticks through a ring of dots, and the drop reveals the full figure.
7. **Guilloché banknote.** Engraved rosette lines in security green with a microprint border. The bass modulates the rosette, the hats flicker the microprint, and the drop prints a second-colour intaglio portrait of a turntable. This is the redemption of Parametric Lines.
8. **Olympic pictograms (Otl Aicher, Munich 1972).** A dancing pictogram set on a strict grid in the Aicher palette of light blue, green and orange. Each beat advances a pictogram through a dance move, and the drop fills the grid with a crowd of them.
9. **Wiener Werkstätte / Secession.** Square-block ornament, gold and black, with Koloman Moser lettering. The ornament repeats, the kick lights one square, and the drop unfolds a Klimt-like field of rectangles.
