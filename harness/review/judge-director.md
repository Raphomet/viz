# Judge: The Director

## 1. Who I am

I'm an animation director. I read every scene as a short film scored to a 124 BPM track, and I ask the questions I'd ask in dailies: does the drop pay off what the build promised, does the breakdown breathe out, does anything have weight, anticipation or follow-through, and is there a character or a camera I care about? Stills can't show motion, so I read it from the run of tiles, the 96 s sheets and each scene's own brief. Where a scene tells a small story in 24 seconds, I score it up. Where it just gets brighter and then dimmer, I score it down.

## 3. My top 10

**1. Mass Poster (`soviet`), 10.** This is the collection's single cleanest piece of animation direction. Two colossal figures hold crossed arms, an X, through the whole intro and build. That's a coiled spring, and the audience reads the tension without knowing it. At 10 s the arms spring overhead and the palms meet exactly on the downbeat. The clasp opens a gap that reveals the sun disc, and a two-second follow-through does the rest: the TOGETHER! slab slides in and sets a letter per beat, the beams go full red, the crowd's hands go up, and leaflets flutter. The breakdown undoes the gesture pose to pose, lowering the arms back to the X, so the whole track is one gesture and its return. On the 96 s sheet the headline word changes each drop (DANCE, MOVE, SING), so the same choreography gets a new line of dialogue every time. That's how you keep a loop from reading as a loop.

**2. Expressway (`expressway`), 10.** This is the one scene where the drop is a *place*. At 10 s the tunnel mouth is a small glowing rectangle ahead of us, full of rainbow neon. We see where we're going before we get there, which is the best kind of anticipation. At 12 s we're inside a rushing cage of light. At 22 s the exit portal frames the city, and at 24 s we're back on the wet road. The staging is entry, interior, exit, the classic three-beat structure of any tunnel shot in film. The kick stays confined: a pulse down one row of barrier LEDs. The 96 s sheet shows each tunnel coloured differently (orange frames, then cyan), so the set piece recurs without repeating.

**3. Zagreb (`zagreb`), 10.** This is an actual cartoon. The little man walks on twos, and the lowered frame rate is a stylistic choice, not a limitation, which is exactly right for the Zagreb school. The build ends with the tram's nose sliding in from the right at 8 s. It's a prop entering early so its crossing on the drop feels inevitable. The drop changes his coat to red, overprints a red moon, pops neighbours out of windows ("oj!", "hop!"), sends a conga line of little silhouettes across, and makes the title letters hop. At 18 s his hat is mid-flight: a squash-and-stretch gag, timed. In the breakdown the conga exits left, the coat goes back to black and he strolls on. The 96 s sheet holds the story every cycle. If I could propagate one thing across the collection, it's this: a protagonist whose state changes with the section.

**4. Chomper (`chomper`), 9.** The chomper and its pursuers are characters, and the maze is a stage that *comes loose* on the drop. The board folds into a sphere, then a torus with extruded walls. In the breakdown it unfolds to a flat maze now full of flowers, so the level remembers what was eaten. The 96 s sheet is the real argument: every drop is a different chapter (a spinning torus, a low chase over a tilted table, a flight down a flower-lined tube, a new level in brown ink). This is the variety-over-time benchmark for the whole collection.

**5. Letterpress (`letterpress`), 9.** It makes anticipation *legible*. During the build, sorts arrive as blind impressions, white on white, so you watch the poster being set before it's inked. At 10 s the platen comes down and everything takes ink at once. That's a perfect drop because the build literally loaded it. Then comes the showpiece: a giant red ampersand behind EVERYBODY DANCE, and a red exclamation mark behind UP UP UP. At 20 s the breakdown feeds a blank sheet, and SWAY begins embossing blind again. It's the most literal exhale in the set, and it's also a reset that re-arms the next build. The 96 s sheet varies the copy every cycle.

**6. Cube (`cube`), 9.** This is mechanical acting with weight. In the intro, the stack puffs idle smoke rings: the machine is *breathing*, a character's idle cycle. On the drop, the radar mast telescopes out, the hazard hatch swings open, big smoke clouds billow and the plunger glows as it bottoms into its rubber boot. That's squash and stretch, named in the brief. In the breakdown the turntable has turned (every 16 s it makes a quarter turn "with a shudder of mass") to reveal the hatch face with a tiny smith inside. Then the hatch closes and the smoke rings return. Idle, action, idle. A room can read the mix on it.

**7. Skyline (`skyline`), 9.** The shell-timing rule is the smartest bit of rhythmic design in the collection. A shell launches on one kick and rises for exactly one beat, so it bursts on the next. Every burst is therefore preceded by a visible rising trail: anticipation baked into the physics. The build is sparse single shells. The drop is a finale carpet with salutes and water glints. The breakdown thins frame by frame (18 s full, 20 s fewer, 22 s one, 24 s one) into gold willows, which is a real decrescendo rather than a dimmer switch.

**8. Thresholds (`thresholds`), 9.** It's structured like a heist film: every door is a mechanism with weight. Through the build an arched door grows until it fills the frame at 8 s. At 10 s steam jets burst on both sides as the latch lets go. At 12 s the whole woodcut inverts to cream daylight in a wave that rushes up the tunnel, and we're through. The gallery of gears and chains at 14 s races past, and by 22 s we're back in the dark with the next door waiting. The drop is literally "the door opens onto light". The steam is the anticipation, and the inversion is the release.

**9. Night Train (`nighttrain`), 9.** This is a classic tracking shot. The train is locked in frame, the world streams past, and the telegraph poles whip through the foreground to sell the speed. The kick is the engine's chuff (a smoke puff, a cab flare, one window blinking), so the plume *records* the beat as it drifts back over the carriages. That's follow-through as a timeline. The drop is a party inside the carriages, with windows turning pink and cyan, plus a bridge over a mirrored river and fireworks over the far village. In the breakdown the windows go warm again and the train rolls under the moon. Over 96 s the sky's palette drifts from blue to magenta to teal, so minute two isn't minute one.

**10. Koi (`koi`), 9.** This one wins on cause and effect. The kick drops rain at one wandering spot, and the rings arrive before the drop (8 s), so the pond is already listening. In the drop, a shoal of small koi swims *toward* the rings, and the characters react to the music's events. That's the difference between an effect and a story. The snare makes one koi snap into a C and dart, a textbook squash on a living body. The breakdown empties the pond and the rain stops. It's calm, daylight, no glow.

Honourable mentions, any of which could swap in: Impasto (a complete sun-and-crows arc), Sumi (the most beautifully confined kick), Murmuration (pure line of action), Constructivist (dancers who exit one at a time) and Futurist (a breakdown staged as aftermath).

## 4. Weakest 8

**Twinkle Toph (1).** The problem: a static dotted portrait with twinkle and nothing else, and no arc across the track. The fix: treat the dots as a crowd with a job. In the intro they drift loose around where the face will be. The build gathers them into the portrait, so the audience sees the face assemble. The drop blows the portrait apart into a spiral following the kick, and the breakdown lets them fall back into place with a spring overshoot. Give the eyes a blink on the snare. The one-blink gag is worth more than all the twinkles.

**Arcs (2).** The problem: judged from the offset sheet, it's a single tiny crescent sweeping in the middle of the frame, alone. The fix: its one-second sweep is an animation engine waiting for a stage. Fill the frame with the grid of cells and phase-offset their sweeps by distance from a point that wanders with each kick. Each beat then sends a wave of closing and opening arcs across the screen, like a stadium card stunt. The drop doubles the grid density, and the breakdown slows the sweep to two bars.

**Rings (2).** The problem: a small dotted target that shivers. The fix: make the rings travel. Each kick emits a ring from a new point that expands outward and interferes with the others, as in Koi. The dots on each ring ride its circumference like beads on a necklace, with their speed set by the hats. Scale it to fill the screen, and let the breakdown freeze the rings in place and dissolve them dot by dot.

**Text (2).** The problem: a logo that inflates on the drop, which is the "centred shape pulsing" failure in TASTE. The fix: make the letters actors. Let V, I and Z each take a beat, so the kick squashes one letter at a time, in sequence, and the phrase reads as rhythm. On the drop, the letters break into their dots and swarm into a new word. In the breakdown they reassemble slowly, with overlap and settle.

**Parametric Lines (3).** The problem: one stunning string-art frame at the drop, and near-black everywhere else. It's all hit and no phrase. The fix: keep a faint version of the figure alive at all times as a pencil line, and let the build add strands one per beat so the figure accumulates toward the drop, as in Letterpress's blind impressions. The drop inks it at full density. The breakdown doesn't cut to black: the strands unthread one by one.

**Low Poly (4).** The problem: the whale is practically frozen in the same pose and place for 24 s, and the promised "flight along the valley" can't be seen. The fix: give the whale a swim cycle you can read in stills. That means a full-body undulation with a big tail stroke on the kick, and the whale banking across the frame over 16 bars rather than hovering. Put the camera on a slow truck so the valley visibly scrolls. On the drop, the calves should enter *following* the mother's wake, and in the breakdown they should peel off.

**Liquid Glass (4).** The problem: too many glass pills over too much type, so nothing reads and the drop is a text swap. The fix: cast one lozenge as the protagonist. Let it roam the marquee like a magnifying glass, lensing one word at a time, with the kick dropping a single bead that merges into it with a satisfying neck and snap. The drop splits it into two that chase each other across the headline, and the breakdown merges them back into one. Cut the pill count by two thirds.

**Grunge (4).** The problem: muddy, mirrored type with no focal point and no motion you can follow. The fix: pick one big word per section and let the bands of type slide *past* it at different speeds, so parallax carries the movement and the word anchors the eye. The kick should rip one band sideways and let it settle back with overshoot. For the drop, flip the ink to one hot second colour. For the breakdown, strip it back to a single band and the speaker-cone scan.

(Also below the bar: Jags and Dot Matrix, both meters with no arc; Diner, where the breakdown equals the drop; and Direct Address, where the headline wall is static and the tear-down lands on a lone "1".)

## 5. Themes

1. **The best drops are events, not levels.** Mass Poster's high-five, Expressway's tunnel, Thresholds' door, Letterpress's platen and Chomper's fold all *happen* on the downbeat. The weak drops (Coral, Iris, Low Poly, Diner) just get more saturated. When a drop is a single nameable action ("the doors open", "the hands meet"), everyone in the room sees it.

2. **Anticipation is the most under-used tool, and where it exists it's gold.** The tram nose in Zagreb at 8 s, the lone gold tower in Iso City, the blind impressions in Letterpress, BOMM forming in Futurist, the halo round the moon in WPA and Bloom, the first raindrop rings in Koi, the purple creep in Aurora and the crossed arms in Mass Poster all tell the room "something is coming". Most abstract scenes have no build signal at all: their 4–8 s tiles are indistinguishable from the intro.

3. **The poster batch discovered chapters.** Penny Dreadful's subtitles, Pop's captions, Suprematism's WEIGHTLESS / IN FLIGHT / PURE SOUND, WPA's "the lake at rest", De Stijl's highlighted schedule row and Zine's page turns all give each section a name. It's cheap and very effective: the copy directs the motion. The abstract scenes have no equivalent, and that's a gap.

4. **Breakdowns split into three kinds: dimmers, exits and resets.** Dimmers (Rave, Mandala, Nebula) just lower the lights. Exits are richer: Constructivist's dancers leave one at a time, Zagreb's conga walks off, Impasto's crows fly out left, Koi's fry disperse. Resets are the best of all: Letterpress feeds a blank sheet, Chladni re-crystallises and Interference snaps clean, so the next build has somewhere to go.

5. **Characters are rare and they carry scenes.** The rabbit, the Zagreb man, the chomper, the rake in Zen, the snail in Clay, the koi, the smith inside the Cube, the dancers: every scene with a protagonist whose *pose* changes with the section ranks high. The abstract scenes that do well (Murmuration, Physarum) find a character anyway: a flock with a line of action, an invading species.

6. **The camera is usually locked.** Apart from the travel scenes (Expressway, Night Train, Sumi, Impasto, Stargate), almost every scene is shot on sticks and dead centre (Iris, Ophanim, Mandala, Plate, Cube, Isle). The two that break this, Mobile's push-in on the drop and Magazine Cover's dancers stepping into the foreground, feel instantly more cinematic. A camera move on the drop is a free way to make it bigger without shaking the frame.

7. **Some scenes remember the track.** Knit's red band, Stamps' print carpet, Zen's leaves, Chomper's flowers and Night Train's smoke plume all leave a trace of what happened. That's lovely for longevity, but it has to be balanced with a way to clear. Stamps never exhales, and Coral grows into a steady state.

8. **Variety over 96 s is the real differentiator among the good scenes.** Chomper (a new fold each drop), Mass Poster (a new headline word), Letterpress (new copy), Night Train (a drifting sky palette) and Expressway (a new tunnel colour) all stay fresh. Stargate and Iris do exactly the same thing every cycle.

## 6. Transplants

1. **The build signal from Zagreb (the tram nose) → Stargate, Nebula, Descent, Mandala, Iris.** One small element should appear in the last bar of the build, promising the drop: a light at the end of the corridor, a single ignited star, one petal of the next mandala. Right now these scenes have nothing in their 4–8 s tiles that tells you a drop is coming.

2. **The coiled pose from Mass Poster → Ophanim, Mobile, Clay, Paper.** Hold a tense pose through the build and release it on the downbeat. Ophanim's wings could fold closed during the build and snap open on the drop. Mobile's top arm could slowly rotate against its balance and be let go. The rabbit could crouch lower each bar before the leap.

3. **The entry–interior–exit set piece from Expressway → Night Train, Sumi, Descent, Nebula.** The train could enter a tunnel or cross a lit town on the drop. Sumi's boat could pass under a bridge or through a gorge. Descent could see its bright chamber approaching before reaching it. A drop that is a *place* beats a drop that is a colour.

4. **Staged exits from Constructivist → Rave, Abyss, Magazine Cover, Clay, Isle.** The breakdown should send characters off one at a time, not fade them together. Rave's crowd could thin row by row, Abyss's jellies could sink out of frame individually, and Clay's critters could pop back underground in a sequence you can follow.

5. **Cause and effect from Koi (fish swim to the rings) → Physarum, Magnetosphere, Murmuration, Bloom.** Characters should respond to kick events, not just to levels. Murmuration's flock could veer around the sun flare, and Bloom's fireflies could swarm to the flower that just flared. It turns a reaction into a relationship.

6. **Chapter copy from Penny Dreadful and Suprematism → Iso City, Isle, Cube, Chomper.** A tiny caption or plate label that changes per section ("Morning tide", "The festival", "Dusk") would make the arcs readable. The toy scenes have the arcs already; they just aren't named.

7. **A new chapter every drop, from Chomper → Stargate, Terminal, Scanlines, Mandala.** Rotate what the drop *is* across a set. Stargate could alternate four walls, a ring corridor and an open star field. Mandala could change its fold count per drop. Terminal already swaps worlds; it should rotate among three.

8. **The idle breath from Cube (smoke rings between beats) → Iris, Ophanim, Plate, Diner.** A slow, secondary idle cycle (a blink, a flicker in a neon tube, an organism's pulse) makes a static composition feel alive between hits. Iris needs a blink more than anything else in the collection.

9. **The blank-sheet reset from Letterpress → Stamps, Knit, Coral, Zen.** The breakdown should *clear* something so the next build has room. Stamps' paper could scroll on to fresh sheet, Coral could let a patch die back to seeds, and Zen's rake could smooth the rings flat.

10. **Mobile's push-in on the drop → Cube, Isle, Plate, Orphism.** A slow dolly in on the drop and out on the breakdown makes the drop bigger without a jolt, and it frees the turntable scenes from their fixed wide shot.

## 7. New ideas

1. **Puppet Show.** Lineage: Paper, Zagreb, Penny Dreadful. The look: a Czech wooden marionette theatre, painted flats, a proscenium arch, visible strings catching the light. The motion: a marionette dancer whose strings you can see go taut. In the build, the puppeteer's hands (silhouettes above the arch) gather the strings, so the dancer is lifted onto its toes and held. On the drop the strings slacken, and it dances with the loose, floppy follow-through only a puppet has. In the breakdown it collapses gently into a sit, and the curtain half-draws. How the music lands: the kick jerks one limb's string, the snare swings the whole puppet, the hats twinkle footlights, the bass sways the backdrop. Across a set, the backdrop flats change per drop (forest, town, moon).

2. **Railway Handcar.** Lineage: Night Train, Zagreb, Cube. The look: flat 1950s cartoon, two little men pumping a handcar along a track through changing scenery. The motion: the pump lever is the kick (a seesaw with real weight and overshoot), and the men bob out of phase. The build climbs a long hill, slowing, with the track visible ahead going over the crest. The drop crests the hill and plunges down, and the scenery speeds up into streaks. The breakdown coasts along a lakeside at dusk. How the music lands: kick = pump, snare = a cow or signal flipping at the trackside, bass = speed. A story in every cycle: climb, crest, coast.

3. **The Lighthouse Keeper.** Lineage: Isle, Sumi, WPA. The look: a flat risograph seascape at night in three inks, with a lighthouse on a rock and a tiny keeper. The motion: the lamp's beam sweeps as a confined light source, and each kick is one flash that catches one boat or one gull. The build brings a storm in: waves rise and the keeper climbs the stairs, visible through the windows floor by floor. On the drop the lamp ignites fully and the beam cuts through the rain across the whole bay. In the breakdown the storm clears, dawn arrives and the keeper walks down. How the music lands: kick = the beam's flash, snare = a wave cresting against the rock, bass = swell height, hats = rain glints.

4. **Kite Festival.** Lineage: Murmuration, Mobile, Isle. The look: daylight on a green hilltop, with bright nylon kites on long lines and a line of children silhouetted. The motion: the kites have real flight physics, with tails that lag and whip. The build is a lull, the kites sagging and their lines drooping as the wind dies (anticipation through slack). The drop is a gust: every kite leaps up at once and the tails snap taut. The breakdown lets the kites settle into slow figure eights. How the music lands: kick = one kite dives and recovers, snare = a tail whips, bass = wind, hats = sun glints on the nylon.

5. **Opening Night.** Lineage: Thresholds, Expressway, Penny Dreadful. The look: a woodcut theatre seen from the stalls, with velvet curtain layers in several planes. The motion: the build raises the house curtain in stages, each kick lifting it one notch while the orchestra-pit lights swell. On the drop the final curtain flies out, revealing a lit stage set that differs per drop (a painted sea, a forest, a ballroom). In the breakdown the curtain descends slowly, the lamps dim and one follow-spot remains. How the music lands: the kick is the fly-rope's notch, the snare flares a footlight row, the hats flicker the chandelier. It's Expressway's "the drop is a place" built as theatre.

6. **Bakery at 4 AM.** Lineage: Cube, Clay, Knit. The look: warm daylight-lamp gouache, a baker, dough, an oven. The motion: dough is the ultimate squash-and-stretch material, and each kick is a slap on the dough. Through the build the dough rises visibly in a bowl (anticipation as proofing). On the drop the oven door swings open with a blast of warm light and trays of bread slide in and out on the beat. In the breakdown the baker dusts flour, which drifts in the lamp light. How the music lands: kick = the dough slap, snare = the oven door, bass = the rise, hats = flour motes.

7. **Paper Plane.** Lineage: Suprematism, Paper, Murmuration. The look: a flat cut-paper sky over a folded paper town. The motion: one paper plane is the protagonist, gliding with a real stall-and-recover cycle. In the build it climbs and slows toward a stall at the top of the phrase, the classic anticipation hang. On the drop it tips over and dives, the town rushing up, and swoops through streets between the buildings. In the breakdown it glides out over a paper sea and lands on the water. How the music lands: the kick wobbles the wings, the snare fires a gust that lifts it, and the bass sets the glide speed.

8. **Weather Clock.** Lineage: Cube, Zagreb, Penny Dreadful. The look: a carved Swiss weather house, with two figures on a turntable (a woman for sun, a man with an umbrella for rain) in daylight wood and enamel. The motion: the build slowly swings the turntable toward rain as clouds gather on a painted sky panel behind. The drop is the storm: the man slides out with his umbrella, rain lines streak and the cuckoo bursts from its door on every bar. The breakdown swings back to sun, the woman returns, and a rainbow is painted on. How the music lands: kick = the pendulum tock, snare = the cuckoo, hats = raindrops. A complete little story every cycle.

9. **Relay.** Lineage: Mass Poster, Constructivist, Futurist. The look: a two-ink Olympic poster, with runners in dynamic silhouette on a stadium track seen in steep perspective. The motion: the build is the approach, with the first runner growing larger and the baton held forward, reaching. The drop is the handoff: the baton is passed on the downbeat, the second runner explodes forward and the stands erupt in a halftone wave. In the breakdown the runner crosses the line and slows to a walk, hands on knees. How the music lands: the kick is a stride with a strong squash on each foot plant, the snare is the crowd wave, the bass is speed. Next drop, the next leg.
